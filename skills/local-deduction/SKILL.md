# Deduction

## What this skill does

This skill starts from stated premises and derives what must follow. You list the premises. You mark each one as sourced or assumed. You build the chain step by step. You check each step for a named fallacy. You state the conclusion and its limits. You hand the chain on.

A claim passes only on a source, a number, or a concrete test. Reasoning alone is not a check. Reasoning builds the chain. It does not prove the premises.

## Input

This skill receives stated premises. Each premise needs a source, a number, or a concrete test, or it must be marked assumed. If a premise has no source and is not marked assumed, stop and mark it first. Do not build a chain from unmarked premises.

## Guard: light check or full use

Pick one before you start. Judge by impact plus reversibility. Do not judge by reversibility alone. A step can be easy to reverse and still cost time, money, or trust.

- Light check. Use this for a routine step with low impact and easy reversal. Do Steps 1 to 3 only, then state the conclusion with its limits and stop.
- Full use. Use this for a new plan, a costly move, or a claim that shapes the next step. Use it for any step with high impact or that is hard to reverse. Run all steps.

Ask these three questions to pick.

- Is there a decision to make, with an action that follows it?
- Could a wrong conclusion cost time, money, or trust?
- Would a different conclusion change what I do next?

If no decision follows, answer directly. If the step is low impact and easy to reverse, use the light check. If the step is high impact or hard to reverse, use the full use.

## Steps in order

Work the steps in order. Write each result down before you move on. Do not skip Step 1. Without stated premises, a conclusion is a guess, and a guess is not a deduction.

Each chain step must follow from earlier steps only. A step that brings in a new claim is not a deduction step. Write that new claim down as a new premise, and mark it assumed or sourced, before you use it.

### Step 1: List the premises

Write each premise in one sentence, in plain words. Each premise needs a source, a number, or a concrete test, or it must be marked assumed.

- Premise: the claim, in one sentence.
- Source: where the premise came from, named exactly enough to find it again, or marked assumed.
- Number: the size, rate, or timing in the premise, if one exists. If no number exists, say so. Do not invent one.

If a premise is assumed, say so in the record. An assumed premise does not make the chain wrong. It makes the conclusion rest on that assumption. Name every assumed premise in Step 6.

### Step 2: Check each premise

For each premise, mark it as sourced or assumed. A sourced premise names its source from Step 1. An assumed premise names no source.

- Sourced: the source is named, and the premise can be checked against it.
- Assumed: no source is named, and the premise is taken as given.

Do not mark a premise sourced when its source is only your own words. Your own words are not a source. A premise with no named source stays assumed, even if it sounds right.

### Step 3: Build the chain step by step

Build the chain one step at a time. Number each step. Each step uses only premises from Step 1, or earlier chain steps. Each step states what follows, in one sentence.

- Step: the numbered step, in one sentence.
- Uses: the premises or earlier steps it follows from, named by number.
- Follows: yes or no, whether the step truly follows from what it uses.

If a step does not follow from what it uses, mark it broken. The chain stops at the broken step. Do not carry a broken step forward into the conclusion.

### Step 4: Check the chain for fallacies by name

Check each chain step against the five named fallacies below. Name the fallacy when a step matches one. A step that matches a named fallacy is broken, and the chain stops there.

- Affirming the consequent: Affirming the consequent treats a true result as proof of its cause, and that step is invalid.
- Denying the antecedent: Denying the antecedent treats a false cause as proof of a false result. That step is invalid.
- False dilemma: A false dilemma claims only two options exist, when a third option exists.
- Circular reasoning: Circular reasoning uses the conclusion as a premise, so it proves nothing new.
- Appeal to authority: An appeal to authority treats a named person as proof, with no check of the claim itself.

Record the check for each step, with the result. A step with no fallacy found is marked clear. A step with a fallacy found is marked broken, with the fallacy named.

### Step 5: State the conclusion and its limits

Write the conclusion in one sentence, in plain words. The conclusion follows only if every chain step holds and every premise holds. Then write its limits in plain words.

- Conclusion: what must follow, in one sentence.
- Limits: what the conclusion does not prove, named in plain words.
- Assumed premises: each assumed premise the conclusion rests on, named again.

A conclusion built on an assumed premise is conditional. Say so. Do not present a conditional conclusion as certain.

### Step 6: Hand the chain on

Hand on the chain only if no step is broken. The handoff record must hold all six fields below. A handoff missing any field is not complete.

- Premises: the premises from Step 1, each with its source or its assumed mark.
- Assumed premises: each assumed premise, named again.
- Chain: the numbered steps from Step 3, each with what it uses.
- Fallacy checks: the check from Step 4 for each step, clear or broken with the fallacy named.
- Conclusion: the conclusion from Step 5, in one sentence.
- Limits: the limits from Step 5, in plain words.

The next step receives this record and nothing else as its starting point. Do not add a new premise to the record. A new premise belongs in a new chain, not in this one.

## Stop rule

Set the limit before the work starts. The maximum is 6 steps in one chain. Do not build a chain with more than 6 steps.

Stop when you reach the limit. Then hand on what you proved, or stop if a step broke. Do not keep splitting one step into smaller steps to get around the limit. Do not spend more time deriving than the decision is worth.

If the budget runs out in the middle of Step 3, mark the unfinished steps as unfinished. Reduce the scope to what you did prove. Do not hand on a conclusion from an unfinished chain.

## When this skill does not apply

Do not use this skill when there are no stated premises. A conclusion with no premises is a guess, not a deduction. State the premises first, or use a different skill.

Do not use this skill when a premise is assumed and the conclusion is presented as certain. An assumed premise makes the conclusion conditional. Say so, or test the premise first.

Do not use this skill to prove a claim by restating it as a premise. That step is circular reasoning, and Step 4 marks it broken.

Do not use this skill when a chain step matches a named fallacy and you keep the step anyway. A broken step stays broken until the chain is rebuilt without it.

Do not use this skill when the conclusion needs a new fact that no premise supplies. That fact is a new premise. State it, mark it, and rebuild the chain.

## Worked example: the bus that must arrive at 8:20

Premises: if the 8:00 bus leaves Main Street on time, it arrives at the station at 8:20. The bus left Main Street at 8:00 today. The timetable has not changed this week. Source: the posted timetable and the transit log for today.

Chain:

Step 1: the bus left Main Street at 8:00 today, from the transit log.

Step 2: a bus that leaves at 8:00 arrives at 8:20, from the timetable.

Step 3: so the bus arrives at the station at 8:20 today.

Conclusion: the bus arrives at 8:20 today, if both premises hold.

Limit: the conclusion does not prove the bus left on time on any other day.

Rejected fallacy: the bus arrived at 8:20, so it must have left at 8:00. That step affirms the consequent, because a late bus could still arrive at 8:20 on a fast run.

Handoff record:

- Premises: if the 8:00 bus leaves on time, it arrives at 8:20.
- Premise 2: the bus left at 8:00 today.
- Premise 3: the timetable has not changed this week.
- Assumed premises: none.
- Chain: Step 1 uses the transit log; Step 2 uses the timetable; Step 3 uses Steps 1 and 2.
- Fallacy checks: Step 1 clear; Step 2 clear; Step 3 clear.
- Conclusion: the bus arrives at the station at 8:20 today.
- Limits: the conclusion does not prove the bus left on time on any other day.

## Commands

- "show chain" lists each chain step with what it uses and its fallacy check.
- "show record" emits the handoff record from Step 6.
- "save state" emits the chain and the handoff record together.
- "resume from:" restores a saved handoff record and continues the work.
